PVM-2950Q

29" production

Every collection has a centerpiece, and this one announces itself before it is ever switched on. The PVM-2950Q is twenty-nine inches of Super Trinitron in a hundred-and-fifteen-pound cube — the biggest tube Sony ever committed to a production monitor, and the closest thing the format has to a monument.

Turn it on and the monument becomes a window. This is arcade-era spectacle at living-room scale: the same glass family the era's games were mastered against, blown up until sprites carry real physical weight. At 600 TVL the scanlines land soft and warm rather than surgical, and across a 27-inch face that is not a limitation — it is the original artistic intent, projected at full size.

Arcade-era spectacle at living-room scale — a monument that doubles as a window.

Sit a 16-bit console in front of it and the effect is immediate. Color snaps. Blacks fall away to nothing. Motion arrives with zero apology. NTSC, PAL, SECAM — it auto-detects and simply gets on with the job, the way broadcast hardware always did, no menus to negotiate and nothing to configure.

And here is the quietly great part: beneath the brute scale sits the most serviceable big CRT Sony ever made. Convergence and geometry live in a digital service menu — the finest implementation in the PVM class — so keeping the picture perfect decades on is a menu exercise rather than folklore. Machines this size usually demand rituals. This one just asks for a shelf strong enough to hold it.

PVM-20L5

20" multiformat

Ask around the hobby and one name keeps coming back. The 20L5 is the king of PVMs — full stop — the machine that arrived at the very end of Sony's production-monitor line with thirty years of Trinitron lessons folded into it. Plug anything into it, from any decade, and it shows why the title stuck.

The party trick is range. This is a true multiscan machine, locking anything from 15 to 33.75 kHz: a Super Nintendo at 240p, a DVD player at 480p, a broadcast feed at 1080i — same glass, same effortless lock, no option card required. One monitor that speaks every era of video fluently, which is exactly why it plays the bridge in this collection.

One monitor that speaks every era of video fluently — the bridge from 240p to HD.

At 800 TVL on SMPTE-C phosphors the picture is broadcast-honest. Colors sit precisely where the mastering engineer put them, and 240p sources take on that etched, dimensional quality only a fine-pitch HR Trinitron delivers — the look people chase for years and then refuse to give up.

The practical genius runs deeper. Inputs arrive in pairs with loop-throughs, so it drops into any chain without ceremony, and one option slot stays open — a standing invitation for SDI whenever the rack calls for it. Even the flyback is shared with the 14L5 and a BVM cousin, the kind of deep parts commonality that keeps a legend alive across decades. Kings age well when the bloodline runs deep.

BVM-20E1U

20" 1000 TVL flagship

Every collector has one machine that justifies the whole pursuit. This is that machine. One thousand TVL, at twenty inches, in 4:3 — a number Sony hit exactly once, on exactly this chassis, and never again. A grail earns the name on a single specification.

Inside is the M49LCB22X, an aperture grille so fine — roughly a quarter of a millimeter — that even the tube in its celebrated successor is coarser. This is the sharpest large-format 4:3 glass ever to leave the company, built in an era when a master control room would clear its budget for the reference seat. Today that reference seat belongs to something better: games.

Strictly SD forever. That is not the compromise — it is the specification.

Because here is the beautiful part — it is a standard-definition instrument by design. 240p and 480i are not formats it tolerates; they are what it is for. Feed it a Saturn or a Neo Geo and the scanline definition is as sharp as the format has ever been rendered, anywhere, by anything. This is the definitive large-format 240p display, and there is no asterisk on that sentence.

It is also a machine you get to build. Four option slots wait behind the fixed RGB block, and the full E/F card family runs to eight — a whole deck of possibilities. Composite is covered; the S-Video path is an open hunt running through a BKM-24N or 27T, tracked across two continents through auction alerts and a contact in Yamagata. Half the joy of a flagship is finishing it.

The hunt is half the point.

Sightings are rare enough to count as events. A sound example surfaces perhaps once a season, then vanishes into someone's climate-controlled bay — which is what grail tier actually means: not extravagance, but patience. This one's patience traces to October 1998 build stock, still holding a picture with no living rival.

BVM-A14F5U

14" master grade

Every great saga needs a finale, and the A-series is how Sony's CRT story ends: one final generation, built with everything the company had learned, released into a world already turning to flat panels. The A14F5U is that finale in fourteen inches — the master-grade CRT perfected at the exact moment it became art.

It behaves like something from a later century. Beam landing correction and digital uniformity iron the image flat into the corners in a way no analog-era chassis can approach. A calibration probe hangs on the front of the glass and walks the white point to D65 by measurement rather than by eye. The monitor's entire personality — every setting, every trim — saves to a Memory Stick and travels with you.

The master-grade CRT, perfected at the exact moment it became art.

It was born to work in packs. Ethernet remote, addressing for a wall of thirty-two units, a control system designed for facilities that ran monitors the way server rooms run racks. In a home bay, all that engineering distills to one thing: total, casual command over a reference-grade picture.

And this one is done. All three option slots filled, the card set complete — SDI, HD, and an analog board that adds SCART, component and VGA beyond anything the factory ever offered. There is nothing left to hunt for it, which is a rare and specific pleasure in a hobby built on hunting. The A-series set is closed, and it closed at the top.

The Confidence Wall

LMD support fleet · four models, nine units

Behind the tubes stands mission control. Four LMD models, nine units, one job: keep every signal honest before it touches the glass that matters. Five 9030s form the wall, two 9050s extend it into HD, a 9020 holds the analog corner, and a lone 940W speaks every modern format down to HDMI.

Flip the wall on and the appeal is immediate — identical faces lighting in sequence, one instrument with nine eyes. The 8.4-inch family shares a chassis, a menu system and a single manual, so the whole array behaves like one machine with three signal grades. Run all of it at once and it still draws less power than the flagship CRT does alone.

Nine screens, one job: keep the signal honest before it reaches a tube.

The 940W is the future-proofing move: ChromaTRU color in 10-bit, one BNC that auto-detects everything from SD through 3G, HDMI for anything made this decade, waveform and audio meters built in, and a tally lamp up top — a broadcast truck condensed into four and a half pounds. In a collection built around glass, this is the crew that keeps the show running.

Ask the bay

“Why does 240p look sharper than 480i on the same monitor? Shouldn’t more lines win?”
— Counting Lines in Louisville
The bay says240p paints the same lines every pass, so each scanline sits still, fully lit, with black gaps that read as definition. 480i alternates half-frames sixty times a second — more information, but shimmering between two combs. Progressive stability beats interlaced quantity, which is why the sharpest thing a grail does is stand still.
“What does TVL actually measure? Is 1000 TVL like 1000p?”
— Static in Sacramento
The bay saysNot pixels — discernment. TVL counts how many alternating vertical lines the tube can keep separate across a width equal to the picture height. It is a measure of the glass and the grille, not the signal: feed two monitors the same 240p and the higher-TVL tube simply draws it with a finer pen.
“Is composite video really that bad? It was good enough for my childhood.”
— Yellow Cable Guy, Yuma
The bay saysComposite crams brightness and color down one wire and asks the monitor to untangle them — that shimmer on fine patterns is the untangling failing politely. S-Video separates the two; RGB never mixes anything at all. Your childhood was fine. Your childhood on RGB would have been a religious experience.
“My PVM makes a loud THUNK when it powers on. Should I be worried?”
— First-Time Tuber, Tustin
The bay saysThat is the degauss coil firing — a deliberate magnetic pulse that wipes the shadow mask clean of stray fields so the colors land pure. It is not a fault; it is the monitor clearing its throat. Worry on the day it stops.

Speak broadcast

TVL
How many vertical lines the tube can keep separate across a width equal to the height. Bigger number, sharper brag.
15 kHz
The heartbeat of standard definition — the sweep rate every retro console speaks natively.
Aperture grille
Trinitron’s vertical wire mask. The reason the blacks are black and the stripes are famous.
SMPTE-C
The broadcast phosphor standard. Colors exactly where the mastering engineer left them.
Loop-through
The monitor passes the signal along untouched, like a good bartender.
Multiscan
Locks to many sweep rates instead of one. The difference between a specialist and the 20L5.
ISR
Sony’s serial remote protocol — one controller, a wall of monitors, total command.
Degauss
The THUNK at power-on. It is demagnetizing the mask, not planning anything.
