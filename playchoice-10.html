Ten Games in One Cabinet

The PlayChoice-10 Cart Wall

Nintendo built the PlayChoice-10 to hold ten games at once, and released fifty-two of them over the life of the system. This is all fifty-two of them — every board shot the same way, front on, filed alphabetically. The set is complete.

The Wall What a Board Is Elsewhere

The wall fifty-two boards

A PlayChoice-10 game is a bare green circuit board with the game’s ROMs in sockets, a Nintendo part number silkscreened down the middle and a printed title label stuck on the front. No shell, no artwork, nothing to protect it — these were never meant to be seen by anyone but the operator who swapped them. Click any board to open it full size.

What a board is and what it is not

The PlayChoice-10 arrived in 1986 and solved an operator’s problem rather than a player’s. A cabinet plays one game; a cabinet that plays ten earns ten times as many chances at the same quarter. Inside is a motherboard with ten slots, and each game is one of these boards plugged into it. Coins buy time, not lives, and the clock runs across the whole menu — pick a game, die in thirty seconds, pick another and keep going until the timer stops.

The hardware underneath is a Nintendo Entertainment System, which is why the library reads like a shelf of NES boxes. It is not an NES, though, and a board like this will not run in one: the ROMs sit behind the PlayChoice’s own security and menu hardware, and the cabinet expects a second screen full of instructions above the game.

Look along the chips on any board here and you will find the same two letters repeated on every one — Nintendo’s internal code for the title, stamped on each ROM so an operator or a repair tech could tell at a glance that all the pieces belonged together. The labels are the quickest way to read a board when the title sticker has come off:

  • BF Balloon Fight
  • CT Contra
  • DH Duck Hunt
  • DW Double Dribble
  • EB Excitebike
  • GL Gauntlet
  • MA Mario Bros.
  • MT Metroid
  • NG Ninja Gaiden
  • RA Rush’n Attack
  • SM Super Mario Bros.
  • YW Captain Skyhawk

The part number tells you the rest. Every board is stamped PCH1 and then a revision — PCH1-01-ROM through PCH1-04-ROM, with a letter suffix — and the later the game, the busier the board: the earliest titles here are three fat EPROMs and a handful of logic chips, while the last ones carry a surface-mount Nintendo mapper and far less socketed anything. Two titles, Captain Skyhawk and Solar Jetman, use a board type of their own, which is the usual reason a set build stalls one or two short of the end.

Signage

The printed sticker that sat on the crown of the cabinet and announced which game had just gone in. Sixteen originals hang on the signage wall — the paper half of this hobby, and much harder to find than the boards.
